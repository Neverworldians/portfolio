How I Turned a Dead Events Program Into a Sellout Machine (On Basically No Budget)

Leadership had killed their $250K events program because they couldn't prove ROI. I brought it back as a self-funding revenue channel.

Events Strategy Audience Development Hospitality Revenue Growth

The Situation

When I joined the company, I inherited the ghost of an events program. A few years before me, leadership had tried using large-scale events to drive awareness for their destination venue, which sits at the center of a multi-brand portfolio including restaurants, a hotel, and meeting spaces. They went big. Each event cost around $250,000 to produce.

The problem: they couldn't draw a direct line from "person attended event" to "person spent money at our outlets." There was anecdotal evidence that attendees were visiting the restaurants and hotel, but no attribution. So leadership killed the big spenders.

The old model
$250K per event. Spectacle-driven. Hope the exposure converts. No attribution, no provable ROI.
My approach
Events as products. Each one self-funded through ticket sales. Targeted at specific audiences with specific unmet demand.

The mandate I got was clear: figure out how to make events work, but make them pay for themselves. Ticket sales had to cover costs. Food had to be paid for. And the scale was mine to define.

The Insight

The previous approach failed because it was built on a spectacle model: spend big, hope the exposure converts. I flipped the logic. Instead of subsidizing a massive production and praying for downstream revenue, I designed events as products. Each one had to be a standalone experience worth paying for, targeted at a specific audience with specific unmet needs.

I'm good at spotting niche communities that have both money and underserved demand. That's a weird skill to put on a resume, but it's the thing that made this work.

What I Built

Themed Afternoon Teas

The first format I launched was a themed afternoon tea series. My boss wanted to price tickets at $35. The LA market for afternoon tea sits around $70, and themed or experiential ones can push higher. I made the case, we priced accordingly, and here's what happened:

Pride and Prejudice Tea 140 tickets · 2x target
People showed up in Regency-era costumes, which means the messaging really landed. Leadership was initially resistant to the theme. I pushed it through because I knew the audience: a massive, passionate, underserved community of literary fiction fans (largely women, largely with disposable income) who rarely get premium in-person experiences designed for them.
Winter Wonderland Tea 150 tickets · 2.1x target
Highest ticket volume in the series. Seasonal timing and theming drove strong demand.
Valentine's Day Tea 110 tickets · 1.6x target
Competing against every restaurant in LA running Valentine's specials, and still cleared target by 57%.

The Murder Mystery Dinner

The second format was something I built from scratch. I designed an original murder mystery dinner experience: three courses, three rounds of clues, printed materials, and a reveal at the end with prizes.

I also wrote the full operational playbook. I mapped the flow of the game alongside the flow of the dinner service, sat down with the F&B team, and trained them on pacing, what to say, what not to say, and how to serve clues in sync with courses. The goal was to make the event repeatable without me running it every time.
